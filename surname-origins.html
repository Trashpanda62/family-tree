The Surnames — Origins & Earliest Records

Where each family name came from, what it means, and the earliest documented bearer on record. Researched against the standard onomastic dictionaries and web-grounded, then fact-checked.

Maxfield

locationalprobable
Maxfield is a "smoothed-on-the-tongue" spelling of Macclesfield — the Cheshire town that was once "Maccel's open field" — carried by families who had wandered from that market town.
Origin: English (Old English origin)
MeaningA worn-down, contracted spelling of Macclesfield, the Cheshire market town. The English Place-Name Society derives the place-name from the Old English personal name "Maccel" (genitive "Macceles") + "feld" ("open country, tract of unenclosed grazing land") = "Maccel's open land." (A variant scholarly reading gives "Macca" + "leah" [clearing/farm] + "feld"; both agree on the "+feld" landscape element and an OE personal name.) Recorded as Maclesfeld in Domesday Book (1086) and Makeslesfeld in 1183; over the centuries the medial syllable collapsed (Maccles- to Max-), giving Maxfield. A folk-etymology deriving Maccle- from "Michael," after the town's St Michael church, is sometimes cited but is rejected by name scholars in favour of the personal-name "Maccel."
SourceThe place Macclesfield in east Cheshire (hundred of Macclesfield) — a habitational/locational surname taken from a single specific named place, not a generic landscape feature.
Earliest documented bearerEarliest WELL-DOCUMENTED bearers of the contracted "Maxfield" spelling: John Maxfield, christening witness at St Antholin's, London, 1539; and Ales (Alice) Maxfield, who m. Robert Hale at St John the Baptist, Chester, 14 Mar 1564 (parish registers; per SurnameDB). The town itself appears as "Maclesfeld" in Domesday Book, 1086. NOTE: earlier "de Macclesfield" bynames certainly existed in 13th-c. Cheshire, but specific names/dates often cited (e.g. "Roger and Gerard de Macclesfield c.1215," "Thomas de Macclesfield c.1239") could NOT be confirmed against a citable primary source — they trace only to user-generated genealogy (a Roger→Thomas, d.1272 chain on geni/familypedia), so they are unverified and should not be presented as documented record.
How it spreadThe "de Macclesfield" byname likely became a fixed hereditary surname in Cheshire/Lancashire over the 14th-15th c. (typical for locational names, as families who had moved away from the town were identified by it), though a firmly-dated early hereditary instance is not established here. The clipped "Maxfield" spelling is well attested in Cheshire and London parish registers by the 16th c. It remains a Cheshire-rooted English surname, never very common; in the US it concentrated in New England and the Mid-Atlantic after early-colonial immigration. Variants: Macclesfield, Maxfeild, Maxfeld, Maxefield (Maxwell is occasionally confused but is a distinct, unrelated name).
To your familyLocational surnames arose independently wherever someone left a given place, so multiple unrelated Maxfield lines exist — there is NO single founding family, and descent from any one medieval "de Macclesfield" bearer should NOT be assumed. The Cheshire region of this family (and the documented Done/Legh of Lyme gentry context) fits the surname's homeland: Macclesfield, Lyme Handley and the Leghs/Dones all sit in the Macclesfield hundred of east Cheshire, so a Cheshire-origin worn form of Macclesfield is geographically very plausible — but that is an etymological/regional affinity, not evidence of pedigree. The tree's earliest proven ancestor is William Maxfield b.1748 Dutchess Co. NY; the chain from him back to a specific Cheshire emigrant (and any link to the gentry families) is undocumented. Note too that some Maxfield-genealogy researchers argue an earlier family byname (Pegg/Peche/de Pecco) may underlie the line — a further reason not to hard-claim de Macclesfield descent.
Your line ends at William Maxfield (b.1748, Dutchess Co., NY) (immigrant ceiling)
The Maxfield immigrant who carried the name to colonial New York is undocumented — his English home parish was never recorded. The Cheshire/Macclesfield origin is the NAME's, not a proven pedigree.

Harris

patronymicprobable
Harris simply means "Harry's son" — Harry being the everyday medieval nickname for Henry — and it sprang up over and over across England and the Welsh borders wherever a Harry had children, so countless unrelated families share it.
Origin: English (and Welsh, esp. along the Welsh border / South Wales)
Meaning"Son of Harry" — the medieval personal name Harry (the everyday English vernacular form of Henry) plus the English genitive/possessive -s, i.e. "Harry's (son)." Henry itself is from Old French Henri, ultimately Germanic Haimirich/Heimerich ("home-ruler"), brought to England by the Normans after 1066. Reaney & Wilson (A Dictionary of English Surnames) and the Oxford Dictionary of Family Names in Britain and Ireland (Hanks, Coates & McClure, 2016) both classify Harris as a patronymic from Harry/Henry. NOTE: the entry's framing of a "parallel Welsh source via ap Harri" is imprecise — Welsh "ap Harri" actually contracts to Parry (and Perry); Welsh Harris/Harries instead arose as an ordinary English-style genitive (Harry + -s) among border and South-Wales families and through anglicization, not from the "ap-" construction.
SourceThe medieval personal name Harry (pet/vernacular form of Henry) + genitive -s = "Harry's son." A first-name-derived (patronymic) surname — not a place-name or an occupation.
Earliest documented bearerUncertain / not reliably established. The widely-repeated "John Harry, 1273, Buckinghamshire, Hundred Rolls" comes from the commercial site SurnameDB, not from peer-reviewed scholarship, and in any case names a man surnamed Harry, not the fixed patronymic Harris — so it is not a true earliest Harris bearer. The earliest genuinely -s form in that same (unverified) chain is William Harrys, 1406, Eynsham Cartulary, Oxfordshire. Fixed "Harris/Harrys" forms are securely attested from the 14th–15th c. onward; an exact, scholarly-verified first bearer was not confirmable from available sources.
How it spreadBecause it simply means "Harry's son," Harris arose independently in many unrelated families wherever a man named Harry/Henry had descendants — there is no single founding family. It hardened into a fixed, inherited surname through the 14th–16th centuries as patronymics stabilized. "Harris" is the characteristically southern, West-Country and Welsh-border form (the north favored "Harrison"). It is one of the most common surnames in England and Wales (roughly top-30; exact rank and counts vary by source), concentrated in Gwent/South Wales, the South Midlands (Worcestershire, Warwickshire, Oxfordshire, Northamptonshire) and Devon & Cornwall.
To your familyFor an English family whose earliest known member is Elizabeth Harris (b. 1759), the name most likely traces to one of the dense West-Midlands / South-West English (or Welsh-border) Harris populations, NOT to any single medieval "first bearer." As a patronymic, "Harris" was coined many separate times, so this family almost certainly represents its own independent line of descent from some local medieval man named Harry/Henry; early records only illustrate how old and widespread the name-formation was — they are not documented ancestors.
Your line ends at Ephraim Harris (b.1712, Connecticut) — bridged 5 generations (bridged to 1712)
Census-proven to Simeon (1816), then FamilySearch's sourced shared tree: Elijah Harris II (1768 Preston CT) -> Elijah Harris I (1739 Salem CT, d.1787 Argyle NY) -> Ephraim Harris (1712-1781) + Elizabeth Brinton. A documented colonial-Connecticut Harris line; below Ephraim lies the New-England immigrant.

Garrison

patronymicprobable
Garrison means "son of Gerard/Gerrit" — the spear-brave man — a patronymic (often carried to England by Dutch/Flemish settlers) that arose many times over, so the name is shared in meaning, not in a single bloodline.
Origin: English, but in most cases derived from a Dutch/Flemish personal name carried to England by Low-Countries immigrants; the root personal name Gerard is Old French of Germanic (Frankish) origin
Meaning"Son of Gerard (or Gerrit)." A contracted patronymic: English Gerardson / Gerald-son, or (per the standard dictionaries, the more common source) an Anglicization of the Dutch Gerritsen, "son of Gerrit," Gerrit being the Dutch pet-form of Gerard. The personal name Gerard is from Germanic *Gerhard = ger/gar "spear" + hard "hardy, brave, strong," roughly "spear-brave." NOT connected to the military word "garrison" (Old French garnison "provisioning/defending force") — that is a folk-etymological coincidence of spelling only.
SourceThe personal (given) name Gerard / its Dutch hypocorism Gerrit — a Germanic *Gerhard, "spear-hardy" — NOT a place or trade. Garrison is the "son of" derivative. The identical modern spelling was reached by two routes that the dictionaries treat as feeding the name: (a) a native English Gerardson, and (b, predominant) Dutch Gerritsen via Garretson.
Earliest documented bearerPatronymics have no single founder. Surname dictionaries (Reaney; Hanks, DAFN) cite the earliest record of the SOURCE name Gerard as John Gerard, 1230, Somerset, England (Pipe Rolls) — he is NOT a Garrison nor an ancestor of this family; this is the source-name citation, not a "Garrison" attestation, and should not be presented as the first Garrison bearer. The contracted Garrison/Garretson spelling is later (the English form largely a 15th-16th-c. Low-Countries import). The best-documented early American bearer is Jacob "Old Jacob" Gerritsen/Garrison of Turkey Point, New Jersey — baptized 1646 New Amsterdam, died c. 1708/09, son of Gerrit Jansen van Oldenburg (alias Gerrit de Mof) — a clear Dutch Gerritsen->Garrison conversion.
How it spreadPatronymics froze into hereditary surnames at different times in different families. The standard authorities (Reaney; Hanks) hold that the English surname Garrison is "in most cases of Flemish or Dutch origin," carried to Britain by Low-Countries immigrants especially in the 15th-16th centuries, alongside a thinner native-English Gerardson line; the colonial-American name then swelled mainly from the Dutch Gerritsen/Garretson->Garrison stream (numerous in 17th-18th-c. New Jersey and New York, e.g. "Old Jacob" of Turkey Point). Because it arose repeatedly and independently, bearers share a name-meaning but NOT a common ancestor. Today Garrison is moderately common, concentrated in the United States (especially the South and mid-Atlantic), with a smaller presence in England.
To your familyFor James Garrison b.1725 in England, the safest honest statement is that his route is undetermined from the evidence here. Note a correction to the intuitive guess: the standard surname dictionaries say the ENGLISH Garrison is in most cases itself of Flemish/Dutch origin (Low-Countries immigrants, 15th-16th c.), so being England-born does NOT make a native-English Gerardson line the clear favourite, nor does it rule out Dutch/Flemish descent — the immigrant-stock route is, if anything, the dictionaries' default for the English name. The "Old Jacob" Garrison of New Jersey line is a separate American branch and should not be tied to James. His specific descent (English region, which Gerard/Gerrit ancestor, native vs. immigrant stock) is undocumented here and unverified pending parish-register evidence.
Your line ends at James Garrison (b.1725, England → North Carolina) (immigrant ceiling)
Came from England to colonial North Carolina; his English origin parish is undocumented (a Dutch 'Gerritsen' route is also possible but unproven).

Davis

patronymicwell-established
Davis simply means "David's son" — a patronymic minted over and over across medieval England and Wales, so countless Davis families share a name but no common ancestor.
Origin: English and Welsh (Middle English / Anglo-Welsh), ultimately from a Hebrew personal name via medieval Latin/French
Meaning"Son of David." A contracted patronymic: David + the genitive/possessive -s ("David's [son]"), commonly via the medieval pet-form Davy/Davie. The given name David is Hebrew Dawid, conventionally glossed "beloved" (linked to dod, "beloved/uncle"). Note: the popular "Welsh ap Dafydd" derivation explains the Welsh strand only; the English strand is an independent English genitive formation, NOT a contraction of "ap." Both converge on the same spelling. (A minor alternative theory ties the Welsh name to the kingdom of Dyfed rather than to David; this is unproven and the David derivation is the mainstream view.)
SourceThe medieval personal name David and its pet-form Davy (Dafydd in Welsh). David spread in Britain after the 12th century — boosted in Wales by the cult of St David (Dewi Sant) and in Scotland/England partly via King David I. There is no single source family: as a patronymic it was coined independently in many places wherever a man named David had children who took his name.
Earliest documented bearerStandard surname dictionaries (Reaney & Wilson; Bardsley) cite the earlier Davy/Davi form in late-13th-c. English rolls: Richard Davi (Suffolk) and William Davy (Oxfordshire), Hundred Rolls of 1273. The fuller -s form is given as John Dauisse (= Davis), 1327 Subsidy Rolls of Cambridgeshire (reign of Edward III). These are the earliest commonly-cited DOCUMENTED bearers in the relevant forms (reproduced from the surname-dictionary tradition rather than re-checked against the primary rolls here); they are unrelated to one another and to this family — a patronymic surname has no single founder.
How it spreadBecame a fixed hereditary surname over the 14th-15th c. in England as patronymics froze across generations. In Wales the older "ap Dafydd" / "ap Dafis" patterns were anglicized to fixed Davies/Davis later — chiefly the 16th-17th c. — making Davis/Davies among the most common surnames of Wales and the Welsh Marches; in England Davis is widespread, especially the West Midlands and southwest. Davies is the commoner Welsh spelling, Davis the commoner English/American one. By the colonial era it was among the most numerous English surnames carried to New England.
To your familyReached this family as ordinary colonial New England English stock: Hannah Davis (b. 1739, Billerica, Middlesex Co., Massachusetts) descends from one of the many unrelated English Davis immigrant lines of the Great Migration era (Massachusetts/Middlesex had several distinct Davis families by the 1640s). Given the patronymic's multiple independent origins, her line must be traced documentarily to a specific Massachusetts immigrant ancestor — do NOT assume descent from any 13th/14th-c. bearer above, nor from the well-known Dolor Davis (East Farleigh, Kent → Cambridge 1634 → Barnstable/Concord) without parish/probate proof. The "Welsh ap Dafydd" story is real for SOME Davises but is unlikely to apply to a Billerica English line absent evidence.
Your line ends at James Davis (father of John Lucas Davis) — confirmed (resolved — confirmed to James Davis)
Primary-confirmed via John Lucas Davis's 1961 Missouri death certificate + agreement across well-sourced trees: father James Davis, mother Rhoda. Our tree's further 'George Davis of Ohio' link is unsupported and now flagged unverified; James's own parents remain undocumented (the true wall).

McCollum

patronymicprobable
"Son of the Dove" — from Gaelic devotion to St Columba; the McCollum spelling marks an Ulster-Scots family that carried the name across the Atlantic.
Origin: Scottish/Ulster (Goidelic) Gaelic
MeaningAnglicized form of Gaelic Mac Coluim, "son of Colum/Calum" — the personal name Colum/Calum derives from Latin columba "dove," the by-name of St Columba (Colum Cille, c.521–597) of Iona, whose cult made the forename popular across Gaelic Scotland and Ireland. In many lines it is a contracted form of Mac Gille Choluim / Mac Giolla Choluim, "son of the servant (devotee) of (St) Columba" — strictly a devotional byname rather than a pure father's-name patronymic. So literally "son of (the) Dove" or "son of the devotee of Columba." (The royal/lay Scots form of the same saint's name is Maol Choluim, anglicized Malcolm.)
SourceThe Gaelic forename Colum/Calum (ultimately the cult-name of St Columba), borne as a personal name and then fixed as the patronymic Mac Coluim. McCollum is specifically an Ulster-Scots anglicization of the name (vs. the Argyll clan form McCallum/MacCallum).
Earliest documented bearerEarliest record cited for the name-form is in the Annals of the Four Masters s.a. 1171, naming a prior of Ardstraw, Co. Tyrone, as "MacGil Colum" / Mac Giolla Coluim (one source supplies the forename Maolmuire/"Mulmurry"); note the AFM was compiled c.1632–36, so this is the annalists' retrospective spelling, not a contemporary 1171 document. Earliest well-attested FIXED-surname bearer of the Scottish line: Reginald (Ranald) MacCallum of Corbarron, named in a 1414 charter by Sir Duncan Campbell of Lochow granting lands in Craignish/Loch Avich, Argyll (verified). As "MacColum" the Ulster surname is listed among the principal Irish surnames of Co. ANTRIM in Petty's 1659 census, and is also recorded in Tyrone and Donegal.
How it spreadA patronymic that became hereditary independently in many unrelated families — there is no single founding family. Two main streams: (1) Argyll/Lorne Scotland, where MacCallum of Corbarron/Poltalloch becomes a settled lineage from the 15th c. (the chiefly line later changed the name to Malcolm, 1799); (2) Ulster, where Mac Coluim / Mac Giolla Choluim families were native and were reinforced by 17th-c. Scots Plantation settlers, with the name a principal surname of Co. Antrim (Petty 1659) and recorded in Derry, Tyrone and Donegal. The McCollum spelling is overwhelmingly the Ulster/Scots-Irish variant, carried to colonial America in the 18th c. by Ulster-Scots emigrants; today it is far more common in the US than in Britain or Ireland.
To your familyFor Mary McCollum (b.1772, Monmouth): the "McCollum" spelling plus an 18th-c. colonial setting fits the Ulster-Scots (Scots-Irish) migration pattern rather than the Argyll clan gentry. Her line most plausibly traces to a Mac Coluim / Mac Giolla Choluim family of Ulster (Antrim–Derry–Tyrone–Donegal) that emigrated in the Scots-Irish wave of c.1718–1775 — "Monmouth" most plausibly Monmouth County, New Jersey, a known Ulster-Scots reception area. This is a regional/cultural inference, NOT documented descent: as a patronymic the name arose many times over, so there is no genealogical link to the 1414 Argyll bearer or the 1171 Tyrone prior — they merely show the name's antiquity. Treat any "Clan MacCallum/Malcolm" descent claim as unverified unless a paper trail proves it.
Your line ends at John 'Mack' McCollum (b.1690) — bridged to the immigrant generation (bridged to 1690)
Moses McCollum (1739 Somerset NJ, d.1825 LeRoy NY) -> John 'Mack' McCollum (1690-1777) + Susannah Wells (FamilySearch shared tree). b.1690 reaches the Scots-Irish immigrant generation in colonial New Jersey.

Hepburn

locationalwell-established
Hepburn is a Scots-Border place-name surname — 'the high burial mound' in Old English — carried by a family on record from 1271 and made famous by the Earls of Bothwell.
Origin: English / Scots (Anglo-Scottish Border; Old English roots)
MeaningA habitational (place) name from one of the Northumberland/Tyneside places spelled Hepburn, Hebburn or Hebron. Best-supported etymology (Reaney & Wilson; Oxford Dictionary of Family Names in Britain and Ireland / FANBI): Old English heah 'high' + byrgen 'burial mound, tumulus' — 'high burial mound'. A common alternative reads the second element as burna 'stream' (Northumbrian/Scots 'burn'), giving 'high place by the stream/water'; a minority gloss offers heope 'wild rose/hip' + burna. The 'high burial mound' reading is the mainstream scholarly one; the others are secondary/contested. It is NOT German or Dutch in origin.
SourceThe Northumberland/Tyneside place(s): Hepburn near Chillingham (Northumberland), Hebburn (Hebburn-on-Tyne, Tyne and Wear), and Hebron (Northumberland) — a man took the surname 'de Hepburn' from whichever place he or his family held or came from.
Earliest documented bearerAs a fixed family name: the Hepburns of Hailes/Bothwell, associated in record with Hebburn in the parish of Chillingham, Northumberland, where the family is on record from 1271 (George F. Black, 'The Surnames of Scotland', 1946, s.v. Hepburn — citing the family's appearance in record from 1271). The named progenitor in Scottish tradition, Adam de Hepburn (also written de Hibburne / de Hylburne), is documented somewhat later: as 'Adam de Hylburne' he and his wife Mariote/Mariota held a charter of Trepprane (Traprain) and Dumpeldar in the reign of David II (1329-1371), per Black — so 1271 is the family-appearance anchor, NOT specifically Adam's own date. The 'saved the earl from a wild horse' grant story is traditional, not documented.
How it spreadBecame hereditary in the late 13th-14th c. as the Border family rose: Hepburns of Hailes, Earls of Bothwell (James Hepburn, 4th Earl, married Mary Queen of Scots, 1567), and many cadet branches (Waughton, Smeaton, Bolton, Humbie, Keith, etc.). From this Lothian/Border base the surname spread across southeast Scotland and northern England, then to Ulster (Scots plantation) and onward to colonial America. Today moderately uncommon; concentrated in Scotland, northern England, Northern Ireland, and their diaspora (US, Canada, Australia, NZ).
To your familyImportant caveat for THIS tree: 'Hepburn' here is genuinely the Scots/English Border locational surname — it is NOT an anglicization of a German or Dutch name. The German element in this line (the Moennig stock from Bakum) enters through a DIFFERENT surname married into the tree, not through Hepburn; surnames descend along their own male lines, so a German Moennig branch elsewhere does not make 'Hepburn' German. Charles Wesley Hepburn (b. 1790, New Jersey) most likely descends from a colonial-era English or Scots-Irish Hepburn immigrant who reached the mid-Atlantic colonies (PA/NJ saw both Ulster Scots-Irish and English Hepburns in the 1700s) — a New-Jersey-born bearer in 1790 fits an established pre-Revolution settler line, not a German anglicization. This specific descent is inferred from the surname + region, not documented here, so treat it as probable rather than proven. Do NOT claim descent from Adam de Hepburn or the Earls of Bothwell: the surname arose from a place, and unbroken documentary descent from the noble Hepburns would have to be proven generation-by-generation, which is not established for this tree.
Your line ends at Charles Wesley Hepburn (b.~1784 NJ; d.1867 Philadelphia; wife Sarah Hopewell) (wall — parentage lost)
Confirmed wall: even FamilySearch's shared tree records no parents for him (it did clarify his wife's maiden name, Sarah Hopewell). A genuine colonial New-Jersey Hepburn; the pre-1784 parentage is simply undocumented.

Mailloux

occupationalprobable
Mailloux means roughly 'the mallet/hammer man' — a western-French tool-name for a smith or clog-maker, carried to Quebec in 1661 by one immigrant, Pierre Mailloux dit Desmoulins, ancestor of nearly every Mailloux in Canada.
Origin: French (western France — Saintonge/Poitou, Oïl language area)
MeaningFrom Old/dialectal French *maillou / maillot*, a western-French form in the *maillet / mail* word-family — *maillet* being a wooden mallet or hammer, *mail* an iron mallet/hammer. The surname is an occupational/descriptive byname for a user of a mallet or hammer — a smith, cooper, paver, or clog-maker (sabotier). Standard reference works (Dictionary of American Family Names; Geneanet) gloss Mailloux as a variant of Maillou, itself a dialect form of Maillot ('big mallet' / a diminutive-hypocoristic of *mail*). NOTE — the entry's claim that the terminal -x is merely an empty plural/genitive 'flourish' (as in Giroux, Marcoux) is over-stated: the more standard lexicographic reading is that -ou/-oux is a diminutive/hypocoristic suffix (so Maillou ≈ 'little/young mallet-man' rather than a meaningless plural). Either way the mallet/hammer root is secure. CONTESTED: a separate Breton (Finistère) homonym is said to derive from *mailhous* 'a dirty/filthy person' (a descriptive nickname); that is a distinct origin and does NOT apply to the western-French Mailloux of the Quebec line.
SourceThe common noun *maillet / maillou / mail* 'mallet, hammer' — a tool-based occupational byname, not a place or a personal name. Same word-family as Maillet, Maillot, Maillou, Mallet/Malet.
Earliest documented bearerFor THIS family the securely documented founding bearer is the immigrant Pierre Mailloux (Maillou) dit Desmoulins, b. c.1632 at St-Pierre de Brie-sous-Matha, évêché de Saintes, Saintonge (now Charente-Maritime), France — son of Jacques Maillou and Jeanne Arnaud; a clog-maker (sabotier, per the 1681 census). He engaged at La Rochelle on 13 March 1657 (3-yr contract) and sailed to New France. First firmly dated New-World family record: marriage to Anne Delaunay, 23 October 1661, in the Québec region (Notre-Dame-de-Québec parish; the act records the ceremony at Pierre's house in Beauport). Confirmed in WikiTree, PRDH, and Fichier Origine. In France the maillet-byname is far older and arose repeatedly from the medieval period; no single original bearer can honestly be named for the European root.
How it spreadIn France the name became fixed and hereditary in the late-medieval/early-modern period across Poitou, Saintonge and adjacent western provinces (also attested in Normandy/Brittany), with several independent founding families — a tool-byname recurs wherever mallets were used, so the European name is polygenetic. The New-World concentration is, by contrast, essentially monogenetic: nearly all Quebec/Canadian Mailloux descend from the single immigrant Pierre (m. 1661). Today the name is overwhelmingly Canadian — Forebears records ~9,460 bearers worldwide, of whom ~6,200 (about two-thirds) are in Canada (heavily Quebec), versus only ~400 in France — the classic Nouvelle-France founder-effect. (The often-quoted '~73% in Quebec' is plausible but more precise than the public distribution data supports.)
To your familyJoseph Mailloux b.1786 at La Prairie / L'Acadie (Québec) sits squarely inside the Quebec Mailloux population, which descends from the 1661 immigrant Pierre Mailloux dit Desmoulins and Anne Delaunay. Given the monogenetic Quebec origin of the name, Joseph is almost certainly a descendant of Pierre through the Côte-du-Sud / Montérégie branches — but the specific 1661→1786 generation chain must be confirmed line-by-line in the PRDH/parish registers before it is asserted as fact (do NOT state an exact 'great-great-grandson' degree without the verified chain). The 'Acadian' framing is geographic (L'Acadie parish, near Montreal) rather than a separate Maritime-Acadian origin: this is a Laurentian French-Canadian line.
Your line is traced to Guillaume Maillou (b.~1475, Louzignac, Saintonge, France) (CONNECTED — traced to France)
Unbroken 17 generations: down through the Québec immigrant Pierre Maillou dit Desmoulins (1632) back to 1475 Saintonge. Ends only at the limit of pre-1475 French parish records.

Thury

locationalprobable
Thury means "the one from Túr" — a Hungarian place-of-origin name, the noble-styled -y spelling of Túri, carried into the Austria-Hungary borderland of Halbturn (Feltorony).
Origin: Hungarian (Magyar); the Th- spelling is Latin/German chancery orthography, not a German root
Meaning"of/from Túr" — a Hungarian habitational (toponymic) surname formed from the place-name Túr plus the adjectival-genitival suffix -i ("from, of"). Túri / Túry / Thúry are the same name: -i and -y are interchangeable, identically pronounced endings (the -y being the archaizing "noble" spelling), and Th- a Latinized chancery rendering of plain T-. So Thury = "the one from Túr." Several places named Túr existed in the historic Hungarian lands (e.g. settlements in Hont County; places along the Túr/Tur river, a Tisza tributary). NOT to be confused with the unrelated French habitational Thury (Gallo-Roman estate names in -acum) — a separate, coincidental name. (Etymology corroborated: Túri/Túry is documented as a Hungarian habitational name "from a place called Túr.")
SourceThe Hungarian place-name Túr (a settlement/locality of that name). The famous noble Thúry line is styled "de Középtúr" ("of Middle Túr") and is placed in Hont County; whether the etymological source village is specifically Középtúr or simply a generic "from a Túr" is the family's own attested styling rather than an independently proven derivation. The surname marks geographic origin, not descent from a single ancestor.
Earliest documented bearerThúry György de Középtúr ("of Középtúr"), b. c.1519 (sources also give c.1520), Hont County, Kingdom of Hungary, d. 1571 (the noted captain of Palota castle) — the family ("Közép-Thúri") documented in Nagy Iván, Magyarország családai czímerekkel és nemzékrendi táblákkal, and in Hungarian-Ottoman war records as an ancient noble family of Hont County styled from a place called Túr. NOTE: this is the earliest WELL-KNOWN bearer of the noble line, not necessarily the earliest documented bearer of the name overall (the name recurred independently); his forebears (grandfather Miklós, father Gábor) are traced in genealogical sources but with less rigorous documentation.
How it spreadAs a habitational -i/-y name it became fixed and hereditary among Hungarian nobility and commoners from the late-medieval / early-modern period (the noble Thúry de Középtúr line documented from the 16th c.). Because it simply means "from Túr," it arose independently in many unrelated families across the Hungarian-speaking lands wherever people came from a place called Túr — there is no single source family. Today Túri/Túry is concentrated in Csongrád, Jász-Nagykun-Szolnok and Bács-Kiskun counties (Túri held by ~3,000+ people in Hungary); the Th-/-y spellings are scattered and relatively uncommon, often in former Habsburg/German-administered border zones like western Hungary and Burgenland.
