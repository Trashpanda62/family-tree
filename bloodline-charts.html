Deep Roots — The Norse North 🧬 ~18% of the family’s DNA
100 BCE – 1050 CESail, Saga, and the Northern ReachViking Age & Germanic Iron Age Norse
Iron-Age Norse farmers, traders and raiders who turned the North and Baltic Seas into highways. They built clinker-hulled longships, raised runestones, smithed pattern-welded swords, and farmed barley and cattle around longhouses. The 442-genome Viking-world study showed Scandinavia was no closed gene-pool: it took in ancestry from southern and eastern Europe during this period and projected Danish ancestry into England, Swedish into the Baltic, and Norwegian into Ireland, Iceland and Greenland. It also showed 'Viking' was a way of life, not a single bloodline.
1,700 BCE – 500 BCESun-Disc, Bronze, and the Painted ShipsNordic Bronze Age
A wealthy, sea-facing chiefdom culture that imported tin and copper from the Alps and Britain and exported Baltic amber. They cast the Trundholm sun chariot, hammered the Tanum rock-art panels full of ships and sun-symbols, and buried elites under turf mounds in oak-log coffins (Egtved Girl, Borum Eshoj). Genetically they already carried the classic Nordic three-way blend, stabilized after the steppe arrival. They passed down a solar cosmology, monumental burial, and a maritime economy that the later Norse inherited.
2,800 BCE – 2,300 BCEThe Steppe Arrives — Cord, Axe, and a New TongueBattle-Axe / Corded Ware culture
The Scandinavian branch (Single Grave / Battle-Axe culture) of the pan-European Corded Ware horizon. Mobile cattle-herders buried under single barrows with cord-decorated beakers and polished stone battle-axes. They carried a large new genetic component — on the order of three-quarters of their ancestry traceable to Pontic-Caspian steppe (Yamnaya-related) pastoralists — that swept into Denmark within a few generations, the sharper of the two turnovers in the 100-genome Neolithic Denmark study. They very likely brought early Indo-European speech, ancestral to the later Germanic languages.
3,300 BCE – 2,600 BCEHorse, Wheel, and the Steppe InheritancePontic-Caspian Steppe pastoralists (Yamnaya source)
The eastern parent of the Battle-Axe layer. On the grasslands north of the Black and Caspian Seas, Yamnaya herders — themselves a fusion of Eastern Hunter-Gatherers with Caucasus and Lower-Volga ancestry — drove cattle, used wagons, and buried their dead under kurgan mounds. Within centuries their genes, and very likely an early Indo-European tongue, surged across Europe into Scandinavia via Corded Ware. They are the ultimate source of roughly a third of modern Nordic ancestry and a major root of the Germanic language family.
4,000 BCE – 2,800 BCEFirst Farmers, Megaliths, and the PloughFunnelbeaker culture (TRB)
Scandinavia's first farmers: they cleared forest for emmer wheat and barley, herded cattle, made funnel-necked pottery, and raised the great dolmens and passage graves still standing across Denmark and southern Sweden. DNA shows a strong infusion of Neolithic Anatolian-farmer ancestry arriving in the North around 5,900 years ago — an abrupt turnover with limited input from local hunter-gatherers, the first of the two replacements documented in Denmark. They passed down domesticated crops and livestock, monumental collective tombs, and the settled village.
9,500 BCE – 4,000 BCEThe SHG — Foragers of the Drowned CoastScandinavian Mesolithic & Pitted Ware hunter-gatherers
The deep native layer: post-glacial hunter-fisher-gatherers who recolonized Scandinavia from two directions, blending Western Hunter-Gatherers from the south with an Eastern/Ancient-North-Eurasian line from the northeast into the distinctive 'Scandinavian Hunter-Gatherer' (SHG) genome. Unlike the dark-skinned WHG to their south, the SHG carried a mosaic of eye colours and lighter skin. They speared seals, fished, and left tools and birch-bark mastic chewed with their own DNA still in it. The later Pitted Ware people of Sweden were a striking case of farmers' descendants reverting to a sea-mammal-hunting forager life.
9,600 BCE – 8,000 BCEMonuments Before the Harvest — Gobekli & Karahan TepePre-Pottery Neolithic Anatolia / Upper Mesopotamia — Tas Tepeler
The far-upstream farmer source. At Gobekli Tepe and the broadly contemporaneous Karahan Tepe — part of the ~12-site Tas Tepeler program in southeast Turkey — people raised T-shaped megalithic pillars carved with animals while still living largely on wild foods. Recent digs reinforced 'settlement, not only sanctuary': domestic structures and grinding stones sit alongside the monuments, and in 2025 the first T-shaped pillar bearing a carved human face was found at Karahan Tepe. The monuments came before full farming, not after. Their descendants' Anatolian-farmer ancestry was carried, leg by leg, toward the dolmen-builders of the Nordic shore.
15k BCE – 9,500 BCEFollowing the Ice NorthWestern Hunter-Gatherers & the post-glacial recolonization
As the great ice sheets retreated, dark-skinned, blue-eyed Western Hunter-Gatherers (WHG) spread up through a re-greening Europe while a related eastern line tracked the forest-steppe. These were the populations that later fed into the SHG. They lived in small mobile bands hunting reindeer, aurochs and red deer, and sit on the western side of the 'great divide' — a sharp Black-Sea-to-Baltic genetic boundary mapped in the 2024 post-glacial Eurasia study. They are the oldest layer whose direct genetic signal still survives in modern Scandinavians.
22k BCE – 15k BCEThe ghost lineage of the frozen northAncient North Eurasians (Mal'ta-Buret')
Deep in Ice Age Siberia, a small population of mammoth-steppe hunters left a buried child at Mal'ta near Lake Baikal — the boy MA-1, whose ~24,000-year-old genome reshaped our picture of the human family tree. The Ancient North Eurasians sit genetically between western Eurasians and the ancestors of Native Americans: one strand of their ancestry flowed toward the first Americans, another fed into European foragers and, later, the steppe herders. They are a faint but real thread running through nearly everyone in this lineage — a population known almost entirely from DNA.
33k BCE – 22k BCEMammoth hunters and the first Venus figurinesGravettian Europe (Vestonice cluster)
Across Ice Age Europe a culturally connected people hunted mammoth, built shelters partly from their bones, fired the world's first known ceramic figurines, twisted plant fibers into cordage, and carved the 'Venus' statuettes that are among humanity's oldest portable art. Genetically the Vestonice cluster is an earlier European layer, largely replaced after the Last Glacial Maximum by the Villabruna ancestry that defines the Western Hunter-Gatherers — but they belong to the deep early-modern-human substrate of the continent and shared symbolic ideas across thousands of miles.
43k BCE – 33k BCECro-Magnon arrives, and the caves come aliveAurignacian — first modern Europeans
Some of the first widespread anatomically modern humans in Europe brought a creative surge: blade tools, bone flutes, ivory beads, the lion-headed figurine, and the earliest painted caves. They shared the cold landscape with the last Neanderthals; the small percentage of Neanderthal DNA everyone in this lineage carries comes mainly from a single, earlier admixture event in the Near East (~47,000 years ago), not from this period. These Aurignacian pioneers are a founding modern layer in Europe, though several of the very earliest individuals (such as Oase) belong to side-branches that left little ancestry in later Europeans.
52k BCE – 45k BCEThe journey that peopled the worldThe Out-of-Africa founder dispersal
The single major dispersal of modern humans that founded every living non-African population is now dated by ancient DNA to roughly 50,000-47,000 years ago — the common doorway through which this whole lineage walked. (Earlier sapiens reached the Levant well before this, around 130,000-90,000 years ago at Skhul and Qafzeh, but those groups left little or no ancestry in people today.) As the founder population moved into Eurasia it met and mixed with Neanderthals — and in Asia, Denisovans — picking up the archaic DNA we all still carry. Every branch above narrows here, to one small band of travelers.
315k BCE – 200k BCEThe African dawn of our kindPan-African origin of Homo sapiens (Jebel Irhoud)
The story ends where humanity begins: in Africa, roughly 300,000 years ago. The fossils from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco — modern-faced but with a longer, more archaic braincase, dated to about 315,000 years — pushed the emergence of Homo sapiens back further than once thought and pointed to a pan-African process rather than a single birthplace. Toolmaking, fire, pigment and the gradual assembly of the modern face and mind all belong to this deepest layer. Every person in this lineage, and every human alive, traces back to these African ancestors.

100 BCE – 1050 CESail, Saga, and the Northern ReachViking Age & Germanic Iron Age Norse
Iron-Age Norse farmers, traders and raiders who turned the North and Baltic Seas into highways. They built clinker-hulled longships, raised runestones, smithed pattern-welded swords, and farmed barley and cattle around longhouses. The 442-genome Viking-world study showed Scandinavia was no closed gene-pool: it took in ancestry from southern and eastern Europe during this period and projected Danish ancestry into England, Swedish into the Baltic, and Norwegian into Ireland, Iceland and Greenland. It also showed 'Viking' was a way of life, not a single bloodline.
1,700 BCE – 500 BCESun-Disc, Bronze, and the Painted ShipsNordic Bronze Age
A wealthy, sea-facing chiefdom culture that imported tin and copper from the Alps and Britain and exported Baltic amber. They cast the Trundholm sun chariot, hammered the Tanum rock-art panels full of ships and sun-symbols, and buried elites under turf mounds in oak-log coffins (Egtved Girl, Borum Eshoj). Genetically they already carried the classic Nordic three-way blend, stabilized after the steppe arrival. They passed down a solar cosmology, monumental burial, and a maritime economy that the later Norse inherited.
2,800 BCE – 2,300 BCEThe Steppe Arrives — Cord, Axe, and a New TongueBattle-Axe / Corded Ware culture
The Scandinavian branch (Single Grave / Battle-Axe culture) of the pan-European Corded Ware horizon. Mobile cattle-herders buried under single barrows with cord-decorated beakers and polished stone battle-axes. They carried a large new genetic component — on the order of three-quarters of their ancestry traceable to Pontic-Caspian steppe (Yamnaya-related) pastoralists — that swept into Denmark within a few generations, the sharper of the two turnovers in the 100-genome Neolithic Denmark study. They very likely brought early Indo-European speech, ancestral to the later Germanic languages.
3,300 BCE – 2,600 BCEHorse, Wheel, and the Steppe InheritancePontic-Caspian Steppe pastoralists (Yamnaya source)
The eastern parent of the Battle-Axe layer. On the grasslands north of the Black and Caspian Seas, Yamnaya herders — themselves a fusion of Eastern Hunter-Gatherers with Caucasus and Lower-Volga ancestry — drove cattle, used wagons, and buried their dead under kurgan mounds. Within centuries their genes, and very likely an early Indo-European tongue, surged across Europe into Scandinavia via Corded Ware. They are the ultimate source of roughly a third of modern Nordic ancestry and a major root of the Germanic language family.
4,000 BCE – 2,800 BCEFirst Farmers, Megaliths, and the PloughFunnelbeaker culture (TRB)
Scandinavia's first farmers: they cleared forest for emmer wheat and barley, herded cattle, made funnel-necked pottery, and raised the great dolmens and passage graves still standing across Denmark and southern Sweden. DNA shows a strong infusion of Neolithic Anatolian-farmer ancestry arriving in the North around 5,900 years ago — an abrupt turnover with limited input from local hunter-gatherers, the first of the two replacements documented in Denmark. They passed down domesticated crops and livestock, monumental collective tombs, and the settled village.
9,500 BCE – 4,000 BCEThe SHG — Foragers of the Drowned CoastScandinavian Mesolithic & Pitted Ware hunter-gatherers
The deep native layer: post-glacial hunter-fisher-gatherers who recolonized Scandinavia from two directions, blending Western Hunter-Gatherers from the south with an Eastern/Ancient-North-Eurasian line from the northeast into the distinctive 'Scandinavian Hunter-Gatherer' (SHG) genome. Unlike the dark-skinned WHG to their south, the SHG carried a mosaic of eye colours and lighter skin. They speared seals, fished, and left tools and birch-bark mastic chewed with their own DNA still in it. The later Pitted Ware people of Sweden were a striking case of farmers' descendants reverting to a sea-mammal-hunting forager life.
9,600 BCE – 8,000 BCEMonuments Before the Harvest — Gobekli & Karahan TepePre-Pottery Neolithic Anatolia / Upper Mesopotamia — Tas Tepeler
The far-upstream farmer source. At Gobekli Tepe and the broadly contemporaneous Karahan Tepe — part of the ~12-site Tas Tepeler program in southeast Turkey — people raised T-shaped megalithic pillars carved with animals while still living largely on wild foods. Recent digs reinforced 'settlement, not only sanctuary': domestic structures and grinding stones sit alongside the monuments, and in 2025 the first T-shaped pillar bearing a carved human face was found at Karahan Tepe. The monuments came before full farming, not after. Their descendants' Anatolian-farmer ancestry was carried, leg by leg, toward the dolmen-builders of the Nordic shore.
15k BCE – 9,500 BCEFollowing the Ice NorthWestern Hunter-Gatherers & the post-glacial recolonization
As the great ice sheets retreated, dark-skinned, blue-eyed Western Hunter-Gatherers (WHG) spread up through a re-greening Europe while a related eastern line tracked the forest-steppe. These were the populations that later fed into the SHG. They lived in small mobile bands hunting reindeer, aurochs and red deer, and sit on the western side of the 'great divide' — a sharp Black-Sea-to-Baltic genetic boundary mapped in the 2024 post-glacial Eurasia study. They are the oldest layer whose direct genetic signal still survives in modern Scandinavians.
22k BCE – 15k BCEThe ghost lineage of the frozen northAncient North Eurasians (Mal'ta-Buret')
Deep in Ice Age Siberia, a small population of mammoth-steppe hunters left a buried child at Mal'ta near Lake Baikal — the boy MA-1, whose ~24,000-year-old genome reshaped our picture of the human family tree. The Ancient North Eurasians sit genetically between western Eurasians and the ancestors of Native Americans: one strand of their ancestry flowed toward the first Americans, another fed into European foragers and, later, the steppe herders. They are a faint but real thread running through nearly everyone in this lineage — a population known almost entirely from DNA.
33k BCE – 22k BCEMammoth hunters and the first Venus figurinesGravettian Europe (Vestonice cluster)
Across Ice Age Europe a culturally connected people hunted mammoth, built shelters partly from their bones, fired the world's first known ceramic figurines, twisted plant fibers into cordage, and carved the 'Venus' statuettes that are among humanity's oldest portable art. Genetically the Vestonice cluster is an earlier European layer, largely replaced after the Last Glacial Maximum by the Villabruna ancestry that defines the Western Hunter-Gatherers — but they belong to the deep early-modern-human substrate of the continent and shared symbolic ideas across thousands of miles.
43k BCE – 33k BCECro-Magnon arrives, and the caves come aliveAurignacian — first modern Europeans
Some of the first widespread anatomically modern humans in Europe brought a creative surge: blade tools, bone flutes, ivory beads, the lion-headed figurine, and the earliest painted caves. They shared the cold landscape with the last Neanderthals; the small percentage of Neanderthal DNA everyone in this lineage carries comes mainly from a single, earlier admixture event in the Near East (~47,000 years ago), not from this period. These Aurignacian pioneers are a founding modern layer in Europe, though several of the very earliest individuals (such as Oase) belong to side-branches that left little ancestry in later Europeans.
52k BCE – 45k BCEThe journey that peopled the worldThe Out-of-Africa founder dispersal
The single major dispersal of modern humans that founded every living non-African population is now dated by ancient DNA to roughly 50,000-47,000 years ago — the common doorway through which this whole lineage walked. (Earlier sapiens reached the Levant well before this, around 130,000-90,000 years ago at Skhul and Qafzeh, but those groups left little or no ancestry in people today.) As the founder population moved into Eurasia it met and mixed with Neanderthals — and in Asia, Denisovans — picking up the archaic DNA we all still carry. Every branch above narrows here, to one small band of travelers.
315k BCE – 200k BCEThe African dawn of our kindPan-African origin of Homo sapiens (Jebel Irhoud)
The story ends where humanity begins: in Africa, roughly 300,000 years ago. The fossils from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco — modern-faced but with a longer, more archaic braincase, dated to about 315,000 years — pushed the emergence of Homo sapiens back further than once thought and pointed to a pan-African process rather than a single birthplace. Toolmaking, fire, pigment and the gradual assembly of the modern face and mind all belong to this deepest layer. Every person in this lineage, and every human alive, traces back to these African ancestors.

410 CE – 1066 CEThe Mixing of Britons and SaxonsAnglo-Saxon & Romano-British England
After Roman administration ended around 410 CE, the lowland Britons absorbed migrants from across the North Sea — Angles, Saxons, Jutes and Frisians — with later Norse and Danish settlement in the Danelaw. They farmed, buried people with seaxes and shields, raised timber halls, and built the kingdoms of Wessex, Mercia and Northumbria. The 2022 Gretzinger study of early-medieval cemeteries found continental northern-European ancestry averaging roughly 75% in eastern England but varying widely by site and individual, mixing with the resident Romano-British population rather than replacing it. This fusion shaped English and the genetic baseline of lowland England.
800 BCE – 410 CEHillforts, Druids and the Celtic TongueInsular Celtic / Late Iron Age Britain
Across Britain and Ireland, Iron Age communities spoke Brittonic and Goidelic Celtic languages, built hillforts like Maiden Castle, and produced the flowing La Tène art seen on the Battersea Shield and Snettisham torcs. They worked iron, minted coins, and made votive offerings at watery places. Ancient DNA indicates this 'Celtic' identity spread mainly through culture and contact rather than mass migration: the population was largely descended from the Bronze Age Beaker-derived people, with a real but more modest gene flow from continental Europe into southern Britain in the Late Bronze and Iron Age. This is the Celtic stratum strongest today in Wales, Cornwall, Ireland and the Scottish Highlands.
2,450 BCE – 800 BCEThe Beaker Transformation and the Coming of BronzeBell Beaker Britain (Early Bronze Age)
From around 2450 BCE, Beaker-using migrants reached Britain from the Lower Rhine and adjacent continental Europe, bringing bell-shaped pots, copper and then bronze metallurgy, archery kit, and single burials under round barrows. Ancient DNA records one of Europe's most dramatic population turnovers: roughly 90% of Britain's gene pool was replaced over a few centuries, introducing steppe-derived ancestry and the Y-haplogroup R1b that dominates Britain today. 4,000 BCE – 2,450 BCEStone Circles and the First FarmersNeolithic Britain (Early Farmers)
The first farmers reached Britain around 4000 BCE, descended mainly from Aegean/Anatolian Neolithic stock who had taken the Mediterranean route across Europe, with a minor hunter-gatherer admixture picked up on the continent. Ancient DNA shows they largely replaced Britain's Mesolithic foragers, and that hunter-gatherer ancestry never strongly resurged here. They likely had darker skin and dark hair and eyes on average, though pigmentation varied.

They worked iron, minted coins, and made votive offerings at watery places. Ancient DNA indicates this 'Celtic' identity spread mainly through culture and contact rather than mass migration: the population was largely descended from the Bronze Age Beaker-derived people, with a real but more modest gene flow from continental Europe into southern Britain in the Late Bronze and Iron Age. This is the Celtic stratum strongest today in Wales, Cornwall, Ireland and the Scottish Highlands.
2,450 BCE – 800 BCEThe Beaker Transformation and the Coming of BronzeBell Beaker Britain (Early Bronze Age)
From around 2450 BCE, Beaker-using migrants reached Britain from the Lower Rhine and adjacent continental Europe, bringing bell-shaped pots, copper and then bronze metallurgy, archery kit, and single burials under round barrows. Ancient DNA records one of Europe's most dramatic population turnovers: roughly 90% of Britain's gene pool was replaced over a few centuries, introducing steppe-derived ancestry and the Y-haplogroup R1b that dominates Britain today. The Neolithic farmers who had built Stonehenge faded from the gene pool even as the monument remained in use. This steppe-rich ancestry is the largest single component of modern British and Irish DNA.
4,000 BCE – 2,450 BCEStone Circles and the First FarmersNeolithic Britain (Early Farmers)
The first farmers reached Britain around 4000 BCE, descended mainly from Aegean/Anatolian Neolithic stock who had taken the Mediterranean route across Europe, with a minor hunter-gatherer admixture picked up on the continent. They cleared forest for wheat, barley and cattle, raised long barrows and chambered tombs, and built great ceremonial landscapes — Skara Brae with its stone furniture, the Ness of Brodgar complex, and the first earthwork-and-bluestone phase of Stonehenge. Ancient DNA shows they largely replaced Britain's Mesolithic foragers, and that hunter-gatherer ancestry never strongly resurged here. They likely had darker skin and dark hair and eyes on average, though pigmentation varied.
8,500 BCE – 6,000 BCEThe Anatolian Wellspring of FarmingAnatolian Neolithic farmers / Fertile Crescent
The farming ancestry that ultimately reached Neolithic Britain arose in central and western Anatolia and the wider northern Fertile Crescent. At sites like Çatalhöyük people lived in dense roof-entered houses, set bull skulls and reliefs into their walls, and buried their dead beneath the floors. They domesticated cereals, sheep, goats and cattle, then carried that package and their genes across the Aegean and Mediterranean into Europe. Ancient DNA shows their spread into the Balkans and beyond largely replaced the local forager gene pool, though hunter-gatherer ancestry persisted and later partly resurged on the continent. These Anatolian farmers are the genetic mother-population of Britain's first farmers.
9,500 BCE – 8,000 BCEMonuments Before the HarvestPre-Pottery Neolithic Anatolia (Göbekli Tepe & Karahan Tepe)
In the hills of southeastern Anatolia, just as farming was beginning, communities raised some of the world's oldest known monumental architecture: Göbekli Tepe and related sites of the Taş Tepeler ('Stone Hills') region, including Karahan Tepe and Sayburç. They carved T-shaped limestone pillars bearing arms, foxes, snakes and vultures, and at Karahan Tepe cut a chamber lined with pillars overlooked by a carved human head. Recent excavation has tempered the old 'temple-only, no settlement' view: cisterns, grinding equipment and domestic structures show people lived and processed food at these sites, not merely gathered to worship. Striking finds continue, including a near life-size human statue at Karahan Tepe. These are monuments built by foragers on the cusp of farming, in the homeland region of the Anatolian farmers' ancestors.
9,700 BCE – 4,000 BCECheddar Man and the First Britons After the IceBritish Mesolithic Hunter-Gatherers
After the last glaciation, hunter-gatherers recolonized Britain when it was still joined to Europe by the now-drowned plain of Doggerland. At Star Carr in Yorkshire they left antler frontlet headdresses, a timber platform and worked wood beside a vanished lake; in the Mendip caves they buried their dead, including the ~10,000-year-old Cheddar Man. His genome suggested these Western Hunter-Gatherers probably had dark to brown skin together with blue or pale eyes — though the researchers stressed the skin-colour prediction is probabilistic, not certain. They fished and hunted red deer and aurochs. Their ancestry was largely overwritten by incoming farmers, surviving only as a small fraction in modern Britons.
15k BCE – 9,700 BCEOut of the Ice: The Villabruna AncestryEuropean Western Hunter-Gatherers / Villabruna ancestry
Before Britain was an island, its foragers belonged to a wider post-glacial population that spread across Europe as the ice retreated. Genetically these carry 'Villabruna-related' ancestry — the source of the Western Hunter-Gatherer component later seen in Cheddar Man. Drawing on Magdalenian reindeer-hunters and other late-glacial groups that had sheltered in southern refugia, they followed game north into a thawing tundra-then-forest Europe. They made fine microliths and were highly mobile. This is the deep European hunter-gatherer foundation onto which Britain's later layers were built.
45k BCE – 15k BCEThe Common Source: Ice-Age EuropeUpper Palaeolithic Europe (shared deep ancestry)
Trace every British layer back far enough and the lines converge on the early modern humans who settled Ice-Age Europe after the dispersal out of Africa. These Upper Palaeolithic people carried a small percentage of Neanderthal DNA from earlier interbreeding, sheltered through cold phases in southern refugia, and produced the cave art, figurines and bone tools of the Aurignacian, Gravettian and Magdalenian. From this ancestral pool later branched the lineages — hunter-gatherers, Anatolian farmers and steppe herders — whose successive waves shaped British DNA. This is the deepest shared root, where the British Isles story merges into the common human past.
52k BCE – 45k BCEThe journey that peopled the worldThe Out-of-Africa founder dispersal
The single major dispersal of modern humans that founded every living non-African population is now dated by ancient DNA to roughly 50,000-47,000 years ago — the common doorway through which this whole lineage walked. (Earlier sapiens reached the Levant well before this, around 130,000-90,000 years ago at Skhul and Qafzeh, but those groups left little or no ancestry in people today.) As the founder population moved into Eurasia it met and mixed with Neanderthals — and in Asia, Denisovans — picking up the archaic DNA we all still carry. Every branch above narrows here, to one small band of travelers.
315k BCE – 200k BCEThe African dawn of our kindPan-African origin of Homo sapiens (Jebel Irhoud)
The story ends where humanity begins: in Africa, roughly 300,000 years ago. The fossils from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco — modern-faced but with a longer, more archaic braincase, dated to about 315,000 years — pushed the emergence of Homo sapiens back further than once thought and pointed to a pan-African process rather than a single birthplace. Toolmaking, fire, pigment and the gradual assembly of the modern face and mind all belong to this deepest layer. Every person in this lineage, and every human alive, traces back to these African ancestors.

They carried the language, the longhouse, and a coastal cattle-and-trade way of life.
600 BCE – 400 CEWhere the Germanic peoples crystallizeRoman Iron Age & Jastorf culture
On the North German Plain between Elbe and Oder, the Jastorf culture marks the point where the Germanic-speaking world becomes archaeologically visible — iron-using villagers who cremated their dead in urnfields, farmed emmer and barley, and herded cattle on sandy heath. As Rome reached the Rhine and Danube, these peoples enter the record as Cherusci, Chauci and early Saxons and Frisians, described in Tacitus's Germania. They traded amber and hides for Roman bronze and wine, fought Rome at the Teutoburg Forest, and slowly coalesced into the Migration-Period confederations. Genetically they sit on a continuum with earlier Nordic Bronze Age and Corded Ware populations, with no sharp break — 'Germanic' is a cultural-linguistic label on a long-resident northern gene pool.
1,700 BCE – 500 BCESun-disc and bronze on the northern plainNordic Bronze Age
A brilliant Bronze Age culture flowered across Denmark, southern Scandinavia and the adjacent North German coast — people with no local copper or tin who imported it in quantity, casting lurs (curved horns), swords and the gilded Trundholm sun-chariot. They raised thousands of turf burial mounds over oak-coffin chiefs (the Egtved Girl, c. 1370 BC, among them), carved ships and sun-symbols into rock, and ran amber-for-metal trade along routes that reached the Mediterranean world. Most scholars treat this as the cultural reservoir ancestral to the later Germanic peoples. DNA shows a population formed from Corded Ware / Single Grave and Bell Beaker roots, carrying heavy steppe ancestry — the same blend the later Jastorf and Saxon-Frisian world inherited.
2,800 BCE – 1,800 BCEThe Beaker remix of the Rhine and the Low CountriesSingle Grave / Bell Beaker culture (NW Europe)
In the wetlands and sand-ridges of the Netherlands, Belgium and western Germany, steppe-descended Corded Ware herders met an unusually persistent resident population of hunter-fishers. The 2026 Olalde study (112 genomes from the Rhine–Meuse delta, Nature) showed that this lowland forager population kept roughly 50% hunter-gatherer ancestry — far more than elsewhere in Europe — right up to about 2500 BC. When steppe-derived newcomers arrived, the two mixed: the resulting Bell Beaker groups carried R1b male lineages and the bell-cup, single-grave, copper-dagger and wrist-guard package, with the local foragers contributing roughly 13–18% of their ancestry. These Single Grave and Beaker folk are direct ancestral stock of the Nordic Bronze Age and the later Germanic world.
2,900 BCE – 2,350 BCEThe steppe pours into the northCorded Ware complex (steppe arrival in central Europe)
Across a vast band from the Rhine to the Volga, mobile pastoralists appeared within a few centuries, burying their dead crouched under barrows with corded-decorated pots and stone battle-axes. They brought a large new ancestry derived from the Yamnaya herders of the Pontic-Caspian steppe — which replaced much of the male gene pool in parts of central and northern Europe — along with Indo-European language ancestral to Germanic. The northern variants (Single Grave in Germany/Denmark, Battle Axe in Scandinavia) are the bedrock beneath every later Germanic population. Recent work nuances the old 'pure Yamnaya invasion' story: Corded Ware men carry mostly R1a (R1a-M417) rather than Yamnaya's R1b, so they descend from a parallel steppe-derived population, not directly from Yamnaya. The lactase-persistence allele was present but still rare here, only rising to high frequency much later.
